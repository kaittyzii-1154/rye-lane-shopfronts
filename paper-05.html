Daily Use of the shopfront Interface

A shopfront is not merely the surface through which a shop displays its merchandise and brand image; it is also a space where daily business operations intersect with street life. Watson’s (2009) study of British markets focused on the role of brief conversations, brief stops, and temporary objects in social interaction; Hall’s (2012, 2015) research on commercial streets, meanwhile, suggests that the business relationships and daily activities embodied by shops cannot be fully understood solely through their architectural facades. These studies suggest that understanding shopfront spaces requires attention to how people use them in their daily activities. In street observations and photographic documentation of Rye Lane, shop entrances, merchandise displays, cardboard boxes, carts, and makeshift shelves recurred frequently. However, visible spatial arrangements cannot fully explain users’ experiences. The same merchandise display may offer convenience in terms of presentation and access for business owners, while it may obstruct movement or encourage passers-by to linger; the placement of cardboard boxes and carts also needs to be understood in the context of how they are moved, organised, and cleared away.

Kim’s (2012) study of pavements in Ho Chi Minh City provides an important perspective for analysing such differences. She points out that disputes over the right to use pavements are closely linked to different economic livelihoods and lifestyles, which are in turn embedded within broader socioeconomic structures. This suggests that differing evaluations of the same space are often related to the activities users rely on it to sustain. Applying this perspective to Rye Lane helps further explain why the same shopfront display might be viewed as necessary for business operations or interpreted as an obstacle to passage. These potential differences were illustrated in the interviews; one respondent mentioned that they “always trip over the abandoned cardboard boxes in front of this produce store.” The word “always” in this account conveys the experience of repeatedly encountering an obstacle and suggests that the shop entrance may have become a location requiring the interviewee’s special attention. The cardboard boxes are thus not only objects left behind by business operations but also, through repeated encounters, contribute to the user’s understanding of this space. Similarly, residents living near the Palais described how the building had been under renovation since the beginning of the year, and the surrounding roads were occasionally closed. In this account, the space surrounding the building is linked to ongoing construction and uncertainty about access. These accounts suggest that while temporary arrangements may not permanently alter the form of a space, they can become part of daily life due to their persistence or recurrence. Consequently, this project views the shopfront and its surroundings as concrete sites for understanding the meaning of everyday spaces: objects, activities, and maintenance processes within the space are imbued with different meanings through people’s repeated interactions and use.

A study by Mercieca et al. (2011) on the redevelopment of the Oxford Circus intersection in London also examined the relationship between spatial changes and user perceptions. Through street surveys and interviews, the researchers compared pedestrians’ perceptions after the redevelopment with those from a pre-redevelopment study and found improvements in ratings related to wayfinding, perceived safety, and environmental satisfaction. This study provides concrete experiential dimensions for examining spatial arrangements. In the interviews conducted on Rye Lane, respondents also described the street through their experiences with obstacles, cleaning schedules, and road closures. These descriptions provide a concrete context for environmental evaluations: a place may feel inconvenient due to objects present at a specific time or because of a prolonged period of construction.

Another respondent noted that rubbish accumulated in front of shops every afternoon and was not cleared until late, obstructing pedestrian traffic. Ramirez’s (2023) report on Choumert Road also documents local merchants’ concerns about how rubbish accumulation and obstructing vehicles affect their businesses. While the specific locations and time periods mentioned in these two sources still need to be distinguished, they reveal that the needs of pedestrians and business owners may overlap: keeping entrances clear affects not only the movement of passers-by but also customers’ ability to access shops. Consequently, the experiences of business owners and pedestrians are not always in conflict; their relationship shifts depending on specific activities and spatial conditions. The duration for which cardboard boxes, merchandise, and waste remain on the street, as well as the coordination between handling and removal, can all alter the experience of using the shopfront. In interviews regarding rubbish, respondents specifically highlighted “every afternoon” and “until late,” linking the inconvenience to the time period during which they had to wait for removal. The significance of the same shopfront also shifts with the activities throughout the day: merchandise displays turn it into a place for approaching and selecting goods; loading and temporary storage alter the available passageway; and items left uncleared for extended periods may lead users to associate the space with obstruction and waiting.

Recognition and Judgment of Everyday Experience

Before experience can change the discussion, it must first be recognized as knowledge relevant to decision-making. In Kusenbach’s walk-along study, a former street lighting department employee paid particular attention to the height of streetlights and their illumination range, while other participants rarely mentioned these details (Kusenbach, 2003, pp. 467–468). This case illustrates how practical experience influences what people notice and how they evaluate their environment. In the case of Rye Lane, the shop owner’s description of reach distances, the business owner’s explanation of fluctuations in inventory, the pedestrian’s boundary within street, and the designer’s analysis of spatial relationships all stem from different practices. Determining which explanation is relevant to a specific problem requires an assessment based on the problem itself, rather than simply ranking them by role.

How objects are named influences which experiences are most likely to be heard. If a cardboard box is labeled “junk,” disposal seems like the natural choice; if labeled “tools,” the focus shifts to convenience in work; if labeled “local artifacts,” preservation may gain legitimacy. Bennett’s research on the relationships between display, classification, and viewing helps illustrate that naming is not a neutral description but rather organizes the possibilities of viewing and handling (Bennett, 1988). However, all three labels may prematurely fix the meaning of an object. A more testable approach should return to usage: what functions the box serves at what times, who moves it, and what impact it has on which activities. The terms “tidy,” “professional,” and “cluttered” also need to be unpacked. Inconsistencies in color or size are visual judgments; unstable stacking, obstructing passage, or increasing the distance required for transport involve specific uses. Kusenbach documented two residents offering opposing evaluations of the same overgrown garden: one perceived a lack of care, while the other saw ongoing maintenance (Kusenbach, 2003, pp. 468–469). While this case cannot determine the value of the items on Rye Lane, it illustrates that judgments about appearance may involve inferences about users and maintainers. For experiences to be meaningfully considered, people should not only be able to express preferences but also have opportunities to explain, when holding differing opinions, what they observe, the basis for their judgments, and the potential consequences. Workers’ experiences are also tied to the often-overlooked labor of maintenance. Mattern’s (2018) discussion of maintenance and care points out that the continued usability of an environment depends on repetitive labor that is frequently overlooked. Displays at the store entrance and temporary storage alike require moving, organizing, and cleaning, but the tidiness or clutter observed at any given moment does not fully capture the process of this work. When discussing whether cardboard boxes should be removed or shelves need adjusting, it is also necessary to understand who is responsible for carrying out the task, when it can be completed, and whether the adjustment will increase the distance items need to be moved or the time required for the work. The business owner’s arrangement of the space may not always align with the staff’s practical operational experience. Discussions about usage should give those performing these tasks the opportunity to explain their working conditions.
