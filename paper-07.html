Participation in Decision-Making

Day-to-day arrangements at shopfronts involve a series of specific decisions: where merchandise can be displayed, how long cardboard boxes and carts can remain in place, how much clearance must be maintained at the entrance, and who is responsible for tidying up and removing debris. These arrangements affect business owners, staff, and passersby alike, but not all parties have the same ability to change them. Since shopfronts occupy the interface between commercial operations and public streets, decisions made by one party may alter the conditions of use for others; their public nature is also tied to how decision-making authority is exercised and how the needs of other users are addressed. The scope of participation depends first and foremost on which matters can be discussed and altered. Cornwall (2008, p. 273) illustrates this by noting that while patients may choose the color of the walls in a clinic’s waiting room, they cannot participate in deciding which services the clinic offers; such choices may have only a limited impact on actual power relations. She also points out that even when institutions invite public participation, discussions may still be controlled by the institution (Cornwall, 2008, p. 275). Applying this perspective to retail shopfronts, the fact that users can evaluate a store’s exterior does not mean they can alter merchandise displays, circulation patterns, or maintenance arrangements. If the issues and alternatives have already been determined by other actors, users’ influence may be limited to choosing within an established scope.

To further assess this scope, it is necessary to deconstruct a spatial arrangement into interconnected decisions. Bratteteig and Wagner (2016) conceptualize design decisions as activities such as creating choices, selecting options, specifying solutions, and evaluating proposals, and they discuss participation from the perspective of power-sharing. Take the temporary storage of cardboard boxes as an example: the decision to remove them also involves where to move them, when to move them, and who is responsible. Users may be able to propose new storage locations, but they may not necessarily have the authority to adjust the timing of the move or the division of labor. The actual role of participation must be assessed in light of which aspects users can influence and whether these aspects are constrained by other decisions. The interconnections among these decisions also link in-store arrangements to street management. de Magalhães and Carmona (2009) discussed the distinct roles of the government, private entities, and user organizations in the management of public spaces, as well as the issue of coordinating their actions. Applying this perspective to Rye Lane, adjusting the storage location of cardboard boxes also requires consideration of the usage requirements for street-facing spaces and waste collection arrangements. Even if business owners and staff reach an agreement, some changes may still require coordination with other managing entities. The authorities involved in day-to-day operations, in-store management, and external approvals must be distinguished on a case-by-case basis. The same individual may have the authority to decide on one matter but may only be able to submit an application or offer input on another; therefore, one cannot presume their influence solely based on their status as a “store owner,” “employee,” or “resident.”

The distribution of decision-making authority is also reflected in the extent to which different actors can set conditions for others’ choices. Taking a retail shopfront as an example, staff may be able to adjust the placement of merchandise and tools, but if the available floor space, storage conditions, and operating hours are already fixed, they can primarily only alter how operations are conducted within those constraints. Store owners can organize in-store operations, but how merchandise is displayed and where items can be placed may also be subject to lease terms and management regulations. González and Waley’s (2013) study of Leeds’ Kirkgate Market documents that lease agreements stipulate requirements for improving stall displays, and placing goods or objects beyond the boundaries of a stall may result in fines. This case illustrates that while displays are physically arranged by merchants, the permissible scope and evaluation criteria can be set by management. Although these authorities are interrelated, their scope of influence differs: some decisions adjust the placement of items, while others specify which spaces are available for use and which activities are permitted. This distinction in authority also affects users’ ability to drive changes to existing arrangements. Gaventa (2006, p. 29) notes that authority can prevent certain demands from entering the decision-making process by restricting participants and the topics of discussion. Applying this perspective to the shopfront interface requires examining not only whether adjustment proposals are approved, but also whether users can propose changes to existing rules and bear the corresponding costs. If discussions regarding entrance congestion are limited to adjusting the placement of items, while storage space and work arrangements remain outside the scope of discussion, staff—even if they participate—can only address problems within the established conditions.

Asymmetry in Decision-Making Power

This distribution of decision-making power also subjects commercial spaces within the same administrative district to differing conditions for participation. Román-Velázquez (2024) conducted a long-term study of Latin American commercial clusters in London, including Elephant and Castle, analyzing developer-led renewal projects supported by local governments, as well as the actions taken by community organizations to resist displacement pressures through collaborative networks. This study links the question of whether merchants can continue to operate to who can drive spatial change, and it also provides a point of comparison for examining Rye Lane. Although both Elephant and Castle and Rye Lane are located in Southwark, the fact that they belong to the same administrative district does not necessarily mean that merchants have the same bargaining power. In the aforementioned case study of Rye Lane, issues primarily revolved around merchandise display, temporary storage, and access arrangements for existing shops; the redevelopment of the Elephant and Castle shopping center, however, involved the retention or removal of existing retail spaces. In response to public inquiries, Southwark Council (2020) noted that the decision to close the shopping center was made by the property owner; at the same time, the council explained that, following appeals from merchant representatives, an additional 200,000 pounds in merchant support funding was allocated. This record indicates that while merchants’ voices can influence adjustments to support measures, the authority to secure relocation support and the authority to decide whether to close the shopping center reside at different levels. The council also plays multiple roles in this process: on the one hand, it drives the implementation of the renewal project; on the other, it assists affected merchants with relocation. From this perspective, participation may improve the conditions under which merchants cope with change, but it does not necessarily enable them to alter the decisions that trigger relocation (Southwark Council, 2020).

Discussions regarding the relocation of merchants at Latin Elephant further illustrate this distinction. In 2016, UCL’s Bartlett Development Planning Unit collaborated with Latin Elephant to conduct a workshop, during which merchants raised demands for affordable rent, high-visibility shopfronts, convenient transportation, and the preservation of the merchant cluster, while also discussing opportunities to return after the renewal (The Bartlett Development Planning Unit, 2016). These demands relate to the viability of their businesses after relocation and demonstrate that the merchants have their own criteria for what constitutes a “suitable alternative space.” However, voicing these demands does not equate to having the power to determine the location, rent, or allocation method of the new spaces. While the workshop report outlines the conditions the merchants hoped to secure, it does not demonstrate that they could demand the final plan incorporate these conditions. This also brings the issue of decision-making authority on Rye Lane into sharper focus: while store staff or owners can move boxes and adjust displays—indicating they can make certain day-to-day arrangements—it remains to be examined on a case-by-case basis who has the authority to approve changes that require additional storage space, alterations to outdoor display areas, or coordination with construction work, and who needs to seek consent. The ability to use and organize a shopfront differs from the ability to alter the conditions under which it is used, involving distinct levels of decision-making authority.
